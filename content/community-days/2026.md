### From Vulnerability Discovery to Vulnerability Disclosure: CSAF Profile 9 for the AI Era

#### Speaker: Vijay Sarvepalli (CERT/CC)

**Abstract:** AI-assisted vulnerability discovery is increasing both the speed and
potential volume of vulnerability reports. This creates a new challenge for vendors,
coordinators, and security teams: how can vulnerability information be efficiently
processed, correlated, and coordinated without requiring humans to reconstruct every
report from unstructured data? CSAF 2.1 Profile 9, the Vulnerability Report profile,
provides a structured representation for exchanging vulnerability information during
coordinated vulnerability disclosure. It captures the information needed to move a
vulnerability report from discovery toward validation and disclosure, including
vulnerability identity, affected products, technical details, evidence, severity,
exploitation, and disclosure information. This presentation explores Profile 9 as a
machine-readable interface between vulnerability discovery and disclosure workflows. It
will demonstrate how AI-generated findings can be represented using this profile and how
the `involvements` structure can provide something like a case ledger for tracking the
progression of reports through CVD. This creates opportunities to measure coordination
outcomes such as dwell time, information gain, and process efficacy as cases transition
between states. The goal is a practical model for vendors, coordinators, and
discovery-tool developers preparing for higher-volume, machine-generated vulnerability
reporting.

### 75+ Vendors, One Standard: What Still Goes Wrong in CSAF 2.0

#### Speaker: Abdullah Malallah (CERT@VDE)

**Abstract:** Writing a valid CSAF 2.0 document is not hard. Writing one correctly, a few
times a year, alongside a day job, is a different problem. We support around 75+ OT/ICS
vendors publishing CSAF 2.0 advisories against a shared template, and what reaches us
tells a consistent story. Publisher metadata, dates are wrong, references are malformed,
and affected products are often difficult to structure correctly. Product tree, version
ranges, CPEs and relationships add another layer of complexity. Because of that we built a
tool that lets partners test their drafts before submitting. Although same mistakes keep
coming back, even from people who have been trained and have the tooling right in front of
them. That is not really their fault. CSAF asks a lot of someone who only writes a
document now and then. In this talk I will share what we tried, what worked, and would
love to hear how others handle this.

**Bio:** Abdullah Malallah is an Information Security Manager at CERT@VDE with over five
years of experience in the IT security industry. Over the past two years, his work has
focused primarily on OT/ICS vulnerability management, security advisories, CVE
coordination, and CSAF.

### From Finding to Advisory — CSAF 2.1 in Practice with `csaf-crud`

#### Speaker: Kyal Smith (ndaal)

**Abstract:** Most organisations already produce the raw material for a security advisory.
It arrives as SARIF from the code scanner, as a finding list from the internal audit, as a
chapter in a pen-test report, as a hit from an SBOM vulnerability scan. What almost never
happens is the last step: turning that finding into a machine-readable advisory that a
customer, a CERT or a regulator can consume without a human reading a PDF. This session
walks that last mile end to end, and shows it running. We start with the process: a
finding enters from SARIF, an audit result or a pen-test report; it becomes an advisory
draft through a GUI form or a single API call; a decision is attached — fixed, mitigated,
or not affected with a reason; and the result leaves the building as CSAF 2.1, with VEX
product status where it belongs, ready for distribution to customers and for the reporting
duties the Cyber Resilience Act and NIS2 now impose. We then demonstrate `csaf-crud`, an
Apache-2.0 Rust tool published on crates.io: a single binary with an embedded database,
TLS 1.3 and no external dependencies. Two views of the same system: the web UI on the
public instance at csaf.ndaal.eu — create, validate, classify (TLP 2.0), export with hash
sidecars and a full audit trail — and the HATEOAS JSON API under `/api/v1/`, where every
response carries its own `_links` and errors follow RFC 9457, so a pipeline can discover
and drive the workflow instead of hard-coding it. Everything shown is open source,
publicly reachable and reproducible the same evening. We close with questions.

**Links:**

- Tool: <https://crates.io/crates/csaf-crud>
- Source (Apache-2.0): <https://gitlab.com/vPierre/ndaal_public_csaf_crud>
- Live instance used in the demo: <https://csaf.ndaal.eu>

**Bio:** Kyal Smith — ndaal Gesellschaft für Sicherheit in der Informationstechnik mbH &
Co KG, Cologne. Kyal has worked as a Senior Linux Administrator for more than 15 years,
running and hardening Linux estates in regulated and critical-infrastructure environments.
His work sits where operations meets compliance: patch and vulnerability handling, audit
evidence, and the tooling that keeps both reproducible. At ndaal he operates and packages
the company's open-source security tools and their public instances, including the

### CSAF in the Wild

### From CSAF Data to Action: Matching Against Your Infrastructure

#### Speaker: Sebastian Wagner (Intevation GmbH)

**Abstract:** CSAF's Conformance Clause 13 defines what a "CSAF asset matching system" is,
but it says almost nothing about how you actually build one. This talk walks through the
DINA Matching Agent, an open-source implementation to satisfy Clause 13 using two existing
systems: NetBox as the asset inventory and ISDuBA as the CSAF documents source. The
Matching Agent is the central link between the data piles, consisting of the matcher, the
asset synchronizer, and the CSAF synchronizer. Further, it extends NetBox via plugins,
adding interfaces to visualize matches, to track the remediation status and adding
required data fields for software tracking. Along the way, the team ran into challenges we
faced, including:

- What actually counts as a "match"? Balancing false positives and missed advisories
- Loose product name matching: CSAF product names, CPEs, and asset names rarely match exactly
- Remediation status handling: deciding whether an advisory revision counts as "no real change"
- Handling data churn: keeping up as new CSAF documents and assets arrive

The talk closes with a live demo of the complete tooling stack.

**Bio:** Sebastian Wagner is a Free Software enthusiast, full-stack software developer,
and project manager. He currently works at Intevation, contracted by the German Federal
Office for Information Security (BSI) to help develop the CSAF standard and its tooling.
With over ten years in IT security, including six years at CERT.at, he has also
co-maintains IntelMQ, a widely used CSIRT automation tool, for 12 years, and is a member
of the Shadowserver Foundation and active in several NGOs for the common good.

### One Package, Many Names: Matching SBOM Components to VEX and CSAF

*SBOM, CSAF and component identification (also relevant to: CSAF is not the only VEX standard)*

#### Speaker: Andrey Lukashenkov (Vulners)

**Abstract:** Matching an SBOM to vulnerability advisories looks like a solved problem:
both sides use Package URLs. In practice, this is where most of the disagreement between
tools comes from. I took one SBOM of an Ubuntu 24.04 container image and matched it five
ways: with four scanners (Grype, Trivy, OSV and Vulners) and against Canonical's own
OpenVEX feed, using the exact-PURL method Canonical documents. The four scanners agree on
53 CVEs. The VEX match finds 14. Canonical's data covers all 53; the other 39 are recorded
against a different version of the same package.

The talk has three parts.

1. Where component identity breaks. Three recurring gaps: advisories that name a single
   version where consumers need a range, binary packages named differently from their
   source (libssl3t64 is built from openssl), and one publisher describing the same
   package differently in OSV and in OpenVEX.
2. What CSAF can and cannot express. For each gap, how it would look in CSAF 2.1: where
   the standard already has an answer (version ranges, several PURLs per product) and
   where it does not (no way to say a binary was built from a source package).
3. What producers and consumers can do now. A short checklist for anyone matching SBOMs
   against CSAF or other VEX formats, and concrete suggestions for advisory producers, put
   forward for discussion with the TC members and tool authors in the room.

Attendees will leave able to find these gaps in their own pipelines and to tell when an
empty result means "no match" rather than "not affected".

**Bio:** Andrey Lukashenkov works on vulnerability management at Vulners, a bootstrapped
and profitable vulnerability-intelligence company. His work sits at the seam where
software inventories meet vulnerability data - SBOM enrichment, component-to-vulnerability
matching, exploitation signals, and the data-quality problems that quietly undermine every
scanner and dashboard downstream. Technical by background and curious by default, he has
unlimited access to the Vulners database and uses it to chase down whatever question he is
stuck on that week, publishing the results on LinkedIn and, increasingly, from conference
stages across Europe. A first-principles thinker with a consistent bias - distrust hype,
insist on measurement, prefer engineering over demos. Happy to argue about any of it over
coffee.
